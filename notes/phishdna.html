~/phishdna

Some notes from building PhishDNA, my CCAI9028 course project.

Background

PhishDNA was developed for CCAI9028 – The Age of Big Data. The assignment was to design and build a data analytics or AI project that addresses a real-world problem in Hong Kong.

I wanted to work on something related to cybersecurity, so I decided to build a phishing URL detection system that predicts the risk of a website based only on its URL.

Why PhishDNA?

Around February 2026, I came across a Threads post showing a phishing website that closely resembled HSBC's official website.

Most of the replies reminded the original poster not to enter any personal information. The poster replied, "Of course I will not! As long as I do not type anything in, I am safe."

I shared the post on my own account and added some thoughts. Surprisingly, several of my friends replied saying they had always believed the same thing.

That was when I realised this misconception is actually quite common among people who are not familiar with cybersecurity.

In reality, not every attack requires victims to submit information. Some attacks, such as drive-by downloads or certain XSS attacks, can already compromise a device simply by visiting a malicious webpage.

That became the motivation behind PhishDNA.

Instead of analysing the webpage itself—which may require loading potentially malicious content—I wanted to see how much information could be extracted from the URL alone. If a URL already shows strong signs of phishing, users can be warned before the page is even loaded.

Original idea

My original plan was for the browser extension to block dangerous websites automatically whenever the predicted risk score exceeded a certain threshold. 

Read active tab URL
↓
Send URL to backend
↓
Extract lexical features
↓
Predict risk score using trained model
↓
Apply whitelist & override rules
↓
Calculate final risk score
↓
If risk score > 75
Block page and display warning

However, due to time constraints and the complexity of intercepting browser requests safely, I decided to simplify the final implementation. 

Read active tab URL
↓
Send URL to backend
↓
Extract lexical features
↓
Predict risk score using trained model
↓
Apply whitelist & override rules
↓
Calculate final risk score
↓
Display risk score and explanation
through the browser extension

Development log

Week 1

  • Collected the initial datasets.
  • Trained the first machine learning model.

Week 2

  • Started testing the model.
  • The evaluation metrics looked great—accuracy, precision and recall were all above 90%.
  • Unfortunately, the model performed terribly on real websites.
  • Almost every URL from my own browsing history was classified as dangerous.

That was the moment I realised a good test accuracy does not necessarily mean the model performs well in the real world.

Week 3

  • Adjusted the scoring logic.
  • Replaced URL length with effective URL length.
  • Limited the contribution of individual features so that no single feature could dominate the final score.

Looking back, I spent quite a lot of time tuning the scoring rules before questioning whether the dataset itself was the problem.

Week 4

  • Worked on the project report.
  • Researched existing phishing detection approaches.

Week 5

  • Finally inspected the datasets more carefully.

That was when I found the real issue.

  • Legitimate URLs were mostly short, clean domain names such as www.apple.com.
  • Phishing URLs were almost all extremely long, random-looking URLs.

The model was not really learning phishing patterns—it was learning that "long URLs are bad."

After discovering this bias, I replaced the datasets with larger and more representative public datasets from multiple sources.

Week 6–7

  • Retrained the model.
  • Refined the scoring rules.
  • Added brand deception detection.

A phishing URL like whatsd-app.com is clearly trying to imitate WhatsApp.

Humans notice this almost immediately, but machine learning models do not naturally understand brand impersonation. I therefore added additional rules to capture these kinds of deception techniques.

Week 8

  • Built the browser extension.
  • Integrated the trained model with the extension.

During testing, I noticed the browser extension sometimes produced different risk scores from the command-line version.

It turned out the browser extension was not applying the hard override rules, such as automatically assigning trusted government domains a risk score of zero. Once those rules were added, both versions became consistent.

Takeaways

  • Building a machine learning model is much harder than simply training one.
  • Dataset quality matters just as much as model selection.
  • Evaluation metrics do not always reflect real-world performance.
  • Phishing detection is closely connected to social engineering, not just machine learning.
  • Sometimes the biggest breakthrough comes from questioning the dataset rather than the algorithm.
