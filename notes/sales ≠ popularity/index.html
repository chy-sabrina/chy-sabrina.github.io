[thoughts] sales ≠ popularity

Quick disclaimer: Notes tagged with [thoughts] are just my personal take. They are not meant to be perfectly written or necessarily correct. I may have misunderstood something.

Where this started

For CCHU5074 Beyond Fake News, we had to write a source evaluation report. We could choose a topic we were interested in, identify a questionable claim, and evaluate the sources relevant to that claim.

I decided to look into K-pop album sales, partly because I am a fan of aespa, a K-pop girl group, but also because I had noticed some things that made me curious about the numbers.

On secondhand platforms like Carousell and Xianyu (闲鱼), I often see K-pop albums being sold for much less than their usual prices. Some listings are for albums without photocards. The albums themselves may still be in good condition, but the photocards have already been removed. Sometimes, the remaining contents seem to have little value to the original buyer without the photocards.

There are also albums sold as "fansign albums" (簽售專). Some of these are brand new and still include photocards, but they are associated with fansign events rather than ordinary album purchases.

And in Seoul, you can even find free albums being given away, often without photocards or exclusive collectibles.

None of these things is particularly surprising if you follow K-pop. But they made me wonder:

If so many albums are purchased for reasons other than simply owning or listening to the music, what do album sales actually tell us about an artist's popularity?

The part that is difficult to know

One thing I have always been curious about is how many albums fans buy to enter fansign events. Some fans buy multiple copies to improve their chances of getting into an event, and the number of albums purchased can vary considerably.

Fans generally do not disclose how many albums they buy to enter fansign events. In the fandom spaces I am familiar with, it is almost an unspoken rule not to ask about these numbers. If someone unfamiliar with this norm does ask, the usual response is that the numbers are a secret. As a result, it is difficult to know how much these purchases contribute to an artist's total album sales.

How many albums are bought mainly for fansign entry, photocards, or other benefits rather than for the music itself? And how much do these purchases contribute to the overall sales figures?

I did not have an answer. The cheap albums I saw on resale platforms and the albums given away in Korea made me suspect that some purchases were driven by these incentives. But these observations alone could not tell me how common this behavior was or how much it contributed to total sales.

Then I came across this interview

While looking for sources, I found a 2022 Korea JoongAng Daily interview with music critic Jung Min-jae.

The interview discussed a question closely related to mine: how accurately do CD sales, music video views, and streams reflect an artist's popularity among the general public?

Jung argued that these numbers do not necessarily reflect how widely an artist's music is listened to. He attributed much of the exceptionally high album sales of some boy groups to fans buying albums in bulk. He also discussed how albums have increasingly become souvenirs for fans, with photocards and other collectibles encouraging people to buy multiple copies.

He was not saying that album sales are meaningless, though. He pointed out that even if ten fans each buy 100 copies of an album, those purchases do not represent 1,000 individual buyers. However, those ten fans are still part of the public, and their loyalty to the artist is meaningful in its own right.

This was interesting because it put something I had observed as a fan into a broader discussion about the K-pop industry.

But the interview also raised a question of its own. Jung argued that bulk buying accounts for a large proportion of some groups' sales. How much evidence would we need to establish that claim?

Evidence that fans buy multiple copies certainly supports the idea that bulk buying happens. But showing that it happens is not the same as showing how much it contributes to total sales. The latter requires evidence about the scale of the behavior, not just examples of it.

What the sources could (and couldn't) tell me

For my report, I looked at sources from different perspectives, including industry commentary, album sales data, surveys, and reports about fans' purchasing behavior.

What I found interesting was that these sources could tell me different things, but none of them could answer every question I had.

For example, official sales figures can tell us how many albums were counted as sold under a particular chart's methodology. But they cannot, by themselves, tell us how many unique people bought those albums or why they bought them.

Similarly, a survey can provide evidence that some fans buy albums to collect merchandise or participate in fansign events. But its findings depend on whom it surveyed, what it asked, and whether the results can reasonably be generalized to the wider fan population.

The interview with Jung Min-jae was useful for understanding the industry's perspective, but I also had to consider how much evidence supported his claims about the scale of bulk buying. Evidence that bulk buying happens does not necessarily tell us how much it contributes to total sales.

This made me realize that evaluating a source is not just about deciding whether it is credible. I also need to consider what it can actually establish and whether the evidence is sufficient for the claim being made.

So, what do album sales actually tell us?

I don't think album sales are a bad measure. If an artist sells millions of albums, that tells us there is substantial demand for those albums. A dedicated fanbase is also a real form of popularity, even if the same level of recognition does not extend to casual listeners.

The problem is treating album sales and popularity among the general public as if they were interchangeable.

A large number of albums sold does not necessarily mean an equally large number of unique buyers. And even knowing the number of buyers would not, by itself, tell us how widely the music is recognized or listened to among the general public.

I started this assignment because I was curious about something I had noticed as a K-pop fan. I didn't expect it to make me think so much about how we measure things.

I think the same issue comes up well beyond K-pop. University rankings, social media followers, exam scores, and company performance metrics all use numbers to represent something more complicated. The numbers themselves may be accurate, but that doesn't mean they tell us everything we want to know.

I guess the question I want to ask more often now is: What does this number actually tell me, and what am I assuming it tells me?
